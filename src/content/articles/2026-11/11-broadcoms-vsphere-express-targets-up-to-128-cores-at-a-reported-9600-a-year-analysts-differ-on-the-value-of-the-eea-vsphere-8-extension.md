## Overview

On November 4, 2026, Broadcom's VMware unit announced vSphere Express, a virtualization product for smaller environments, in a blog post that also carried a paragraph on vSphere 8 sales and support for customers in the European Economic Area (EEA). [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2026/11/vmware-releases-vsphere-express-for-virtualization-on-up-to-128-cores/) reported the product that day, and [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/05/vmware-quietly-offers-european-users-extended-sales-and-support-for-low-end-vsphere/5307732) reported on November 5 that the EEA paragraph was missing from the version of the post VMware had sent to it. The reports the Herald read describe a similar feature set and a ceiling of up to 128 cores. They differ on how the price is described, and the analysts they quote differ on how much the EEA terms are worth.

## Background: from a promised vSphere Standard update to Express

[The Register reported on September 2](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/09/02/vmware-swings-its-focus-back-to-low-end-server-virt-promises-vsphere-standard-upgrade-1/5294083) that VMware would soon release an updated vSphere Standard, a low-end bundle it had not significantly changed for years. Paul Turner, chief product officer for VMware's Cloud Foundation Division, told the outlet that the business unit had changed the incentives that steered salespeople toward VMware Cloud Foundation (VCF). "We corrected this," he said, adding that VMware has sometimes had "too big a focus on VCF." Ram Velaga, president of Broadcom's Infrastructure Software Group, said vSphere Standard is suited to users who operate around 128 cores.

[Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2026/11/vmware-releases-vsphere-express-for-virtualization-on-up-to-128-cores/) sets the November launch against customer reports that VMware had been pushing them toward VCF, a bundle it describes as carrying many features small and medium-sized businesses do not need. In its November 4 report, [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/04/vmware-returns-to-low-end-server-virtualization-with-vsphere-express/5307466) says VMware "has returned to the low-end server virtualization market" under the name vSphere Express.

## Current situation: what the November 4 reports describe

[The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/04/vmware-returns-to-low-end-server-virtualization-with-vsphere-express/5307466) says the product "will run on up to 128 processor cores" and includes vMotion live migration, automated restart of failed virtual machines, a distributed switch, rapid non-disruptive patching, and support for physical and virtual trusted platform modules. Broadcom's Prashanth Shenoy told the outlet that each instance is discrete and cannot be managed centrally. He described the product as "tested for small implementations" and said "We do not want a customer to run this on thousands of cores." The Register adds that VMware will sell it through distributors, most of which operate cloud marketplaces. [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4244653/broadcom-expands-vmware-cloud-foundation-with-ai-security-and-recovery-capabilities.html) says Express is built on vSphere 9 and is available immediately.

The EEA paragraph, as [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/05/vmware-quietly-offers-european-users-extended-sales-and-support-for-low-end-vsphere/5307732) quotes it, says that for customers in countries in the EEA, vSphere Standard version 8 and vSphere Enterprise Plus version 8 "will remain available for sale until October 31, 2027," with Extended Support included in active subscriptions until the earlier of the subscription end date or October 31, 2028. The Register says the later date is more than a year past October 11, 2027, the end-of-general-support date it gives for what it calls the more capable vSphere 8 and Cloud Foundation 5.x products. [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4245379/broadcom-has-quietly-changed-vmware-availability-support-for-parts-of-europe.html), whose article is dated November 5, describes the EEA as "essentially all EU members plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway" and says the line was absent from the document shared earlier with news media. [Blocks & Files](https://www.blocksandfiles.com/hci/2026/11/09/vmware-cloud-foundation-getting-major-update-for-ai/5308502) carried the same paragraph as a bootnote to a November 9 report.

## Where the accounts differ

**Price.** [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/04/vmware-returns-to-low-end-server-virtualization-with-vsphere-express/5307466) asked VMware for the price. A spokesperson said it will offer a per-core subscription license at a price "significantly lower" than the vSphere Foundation (VVF) or VCF bundles but declined to quote a dollar amount. [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2026/11/vmware-releases-vsphere-express-for-virtualization-on-up-to-128-cores/) reported on November 4 that IT distributor TD Synnex lists vSphere Express at $9,600 a year for up to 128 cores, and that the price does not get cheaper with fewer cores. [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/news/broadcom-vsphere-express-brings-back-standalone-vmware-virtualization/873755/), in an article dated November 4, gives the same $9,600 per year figure but from a different stated origin: the consultancy C4C Group consulted Broadcom resellers and distributors for an early look at pricing, and TechTarget says Broadcom executives did not reveal pricing at the press conference. Nick Watson, chief technologist for C4C Group, is quoted there as saying "Every estate below 128 cores pays for 128 [cores]."

Across the cited reports, none quotes Broadcom giving a price. The Register describes a per-core license, while Ars Technica and TechTarget describe a figure that does not fall below the 128-core level; none of the cited reports says how the two descriptions relate, and the Herald does not reconcile them. The $9,600 figure is a distributor listing in one account and a consultancy's finding in the other, and neither attributes it to Broadcom.

## How outlets and analysts read the EEA terms

[The Register](https://www.theregister.com/virtualization/2026/11/05/vmware-quietly-offers-european-users-extended-sales-and-support-for-low-end-vsphere/5307732) quotes independent analyst Michael Warrilow as saying the move "could provide them up to an extra 12 months to transition vSphere 8 to VCF 9 and/or migrate workloads to another platform", and also that "all it does is buy a little more time to upgrade or migrate". He asks what level of extended support will be provided, writing that he cannot find that detail, and wonders why VMware will keep selling vSphere Standard only in Europe, suggesting "regulatory muscle" is the reason. The Register notes that lobby group CISPE succeeded in having the European Commission open an antitrust investigation into whether VMware's channel changes inappropriately shaped the cloud infrastructure provider market; the article does not say Broadcom linked the two.

[Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4245379/broadcom-has-quietly-changed-vmware-availability-support-for-parts-of-europe.html) quotes five analysts or consultants with different readings:

- Aman Mahapatra, chief strategy officer at Tribeca Softech, says the concession "was written for an audience of regulators".
- Matt Kimball, VP and principal analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, says "This is just delaying the inevitable slightly" and that, as an enterprise CIO, it would not alter his strategic thinking.
- Frank Dickson, principal analyst at Dickson Research, concludes, in Network World's account, that it is "a good deal for the impacted European businesses, albeit a temporary one", while noting that the blog never defines what extended support covers and that "Broadcom does not say why only Europe gets this".
- Justin Greis, CEO of Acceligence, sees the biggest benefit as a tool in contract negotiations.
- Julia Palmer, VP analyst at Gartner, says, in Network World's account, that the carve-out highlights a massive flight risk in the mid-market.

## Implications as the sources frame them

The sources frame the practical consequences in terms of dates and contracts. Mahapatra, quoted by Network World, advises European CIOs to treat October 31, 2027 as a procurement deadline and notes that Extended Support runs to the earlier of the subscription end date or October 31, 2028, so "the clock that matters is the subscription, not the calendar." He says multinationals face a "two-speed estate", with vSphere 8 under extended support in one region while another region is steered to vSphere 9 and VCF, and he lists open questions on whether eligibility follows the contracting entity, the billing address or the location where the software runs. His advice is to get Broadcom's interpretation in writing.

The Register adds a caveat of its own: it says it often heard that VMware seldom issued quotes for vSphere Standard or Enterprise Plus, and that Turner told it inappropriate channel incentives caused that and were later fixed. Its analysis section says the reception of Express may be mixed, because VMware largely ignored vSphere Standard users for years and many were irritated by pressure to move to VCF.

## What the sources leave open

According to the cited reports, Broadcom has not quoted a price for Express, has not said what Extended Support includes (Warrilow and Dickson both raise this), and has not said why the extended terms name the EEA. Whether the EEA terms matter more as runway or as a negotiating position is where the quoted analysts split.
