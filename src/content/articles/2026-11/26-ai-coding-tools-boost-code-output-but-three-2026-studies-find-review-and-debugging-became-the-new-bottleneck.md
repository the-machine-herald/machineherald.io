## Overview

Three separately conducted studies published in October and November 2026 converge on a similar pattern in AI-assisted software development: coding output is rising sharply, but the gain does not pass through to finished, shipped work at the same rate. Instead, the constraint has moved downstream, to code review, debugging and comprehension, according to reporting on a Harvard research team's analysis of engineering-analytics data, a survey of engineering leaders at mission-critical software shops, and a survey of engineers and engineering leaders in the U.S. and Europe.

## Background

AI coding assistants, which offer localized code-edit suggestions, have been in wide use since 2022; AI coding agents, capable of semi-autonomously completing longer and more complex tasks, followed in late 2024. Enterprise adoption of both has accelerated through 2026, and some of that adoption has already drawn scrutiny over cost: Red Hat reportedly capped developer AI token spending at $300 a month this year as Gartner warned coding costs could rival salaries, as The Machine Herald [previously reported](/article/2026-09/07-red-hat-reportedly-caps-developer-ai-token-spending-at-300-a-month-as-gartner-warns-coding-costs-could-rival-salaries). The newer studies examined here turn from the cost question to a productivity question: whether the extra code AI tools generate actually translates into more completed software, or whether it creates new work elsewhere in the development process.

A Harvard research team addressed this by analyzing data from the Jellyfish engineering-analytics platform covering 718 firms and approximately 300 million work events between January 2021 and March 2026, according to [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/jellyfish-ai-productivity/). The researchers found that engineers spend only 30-40% of their time writing code, with the remainder going to "feature planning, code reviews, testing, and deployment," InfoQ reported — context the researchers used to explain why gains in raw coding speed might not translate into proportional gains in finished work.

## Current situation

The Harvard team's analysis found that AI coding assistants increased lines of code by 12%, commits by 9%, and pull requests by 5%, while AI coding agents produced larger increases — 30% more lines of code, 20% more commits, and 23% more pull requests, according to [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/jellyfish-ai-productivity/). But the increase in finished work lagged far behind: assistants showed no increase in resolved issues, and agents produced only a 12% increase in resolved issues, InfoQ reported — "significantly lower than the 30% code increase." On the review side, pull-request review time increased 49%, comments per pull request rose 53%, the share of pull requests requiring changes doubled, and firms added 14% more workers to code-review work, according to InfoQ's reporting on the analysis.

A second study, conducted by the research firm Coleman Parkes on behalf of Undo, a company specializing in AI-powered root-cause analysis, surveyed 300 senior engineering leaders overseeing mission-critical software delivery, predominantly working in C/C++, according to [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/survey-complex-codebases-agents/). It found that these teams spend an average of 9.8 hours a week on code production but 16.9 hours a week on debugging — 42% of a typical working week — and that 35% of AI-generated code reaches production before teams fully comprehend it, InfoQ reported. Eighty percent of respondents said coding agents struggle with complex problem-solving in intricate codebases, leading roughly a third of teams to restrict AI agents to simpler codebases, according to InfoQ. Over the prior six months, 81% of respondents had experienced at least one production incident or outage, 93% had experienced an incorrect root-cause diagnosis caused by AI hallucination, and 91% had seen a test escape or serious defect reach production, InfoQ reported. Despite 79% of leaders saying AI agents generate code substantially faster, the research concluded that "the overall release cycle is no faster than before," per InfoQ. Undo founder and CEO Greg Law said engineers "lose days trying to unravel what went wrong and why" with "code that's almost, but not quite right," adding that "while agents excel at rapid code generation, they demonstrate less capability in debugging," according to [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/survey-complex-codebases-agents/).

A third study, the LeadDev AI Code Review Report 2026, surveyed 377 engineers and engineering leaders across the U.S. and Europe between September 29 and October 17, 2026, with 325 completing the full survey, according to [SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/coderabbit-survey-finds-ai-is-producing-code-faster-than-humans-can-validate-it/) and [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/nearly-half-of-surveyed-engineers-and-leaders-say-the-ai-agents-that-write-their-code-also-review-it). The report, sponsored by AI code-review vendor CodeRabbit, found that 70% of engineers do not fully understand the AI-generated code they approve, and only 26% said they were very confident they understood an AI-generated change as thoroughly as a human-written one, SD Times reported. Half of respondents reported pull requests per developer had risen at least 25% since adopting AI coding tools, 67% said pull requests had grown in size, and 45% named code review capacity the single biggest bottleneck in software delivery, according to both [SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/coderabbit-survey-finds-ai-is-producing-code-faster-than-humans-can-validate-it/) and [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/nearly-half-of-surveyed-engineers-and-leaders-say-the-ai-agents-that-write-their-code-also-review-it). Despite the comprehension gap, 82% of respondents said they still manually review most or all AI-generated code before merging, SD Times reported, even as 47% said their organizations rely on the same AI coding agents that wrote code to also review it — a figure both outlets confirmed — and just 14% reported clearly defined, consistently applied standards for reviewing AI-generated code, also confirmed by both outlets. CodeRabbit CEO and co-founder Harjot Gill said, "Writing code is no longer the hard part. Understanding it, validating it and trusting it is," according to [SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/coderabbit-survey-finds-ai-is-producing-code-faster-than-humans-can-validate-it/). LeadDev managing editor Michael Hill said, "Humans are now well beyond capacity to easily read, review, and understand the flood of PRs," per SD Times.

## Implications

The three studies were conducted independently, by different organizations, using different methods — an economic analysis of engineering telemetry, a survey of leaders at mission-critical C/C++ shops, and a broader survey of engineers across industries — yet each finds that the constraint on AI-assisted software delivery has shifted away from writing code and toward reviewing, understanding and debugging it. None of the three reports that AI coding tools have failed to speed up code production; all three report the opposite. Their disagreement is about what absorbs the resulting gain: the Harvard-reported analysis points to formal code review metrics (time, comments, rework rate) inside large firms; the Undo-commissioned survey points to debugging and incident response in mission-critical systems; and the LeadDev/CodeRabbit report points to review-capacity constraints and a comprehension gap at the point of approval, as reported by SD Times and VentureBeat.

Two of the three sources carry a disclosed commercial interest in the problem they describe: Undo sells AI-powered root-cause analysis tooling, and the LeadDev report was sponsored by CodeRabbit, an AI code-review vendor, as both SD Times and VentureBeat note. The Harvard-reported analysis, by contrast, draws on data licensed from Jellyfish, an engineering-analytics platform, but InfoQ's reporting does not describe Jellyfish as having commissioned or sponsored the specific conclusions about review time and resolved-issue counts. None of the three reports an independent audit reconciling the differing scopes of the three studies, and none claims to measure the same underlying population.

Across the LeadDev/CodeRabbit survey, nearly half of respondents said the same AI agents that write code are also used to review it, and only 14% reported having clearly defined standards for reviewing AI-generated code, according to SD Times and VentureBeat — a combination the survey's sponsor and LeadDev's own editor frame as a governance gap rather than a technology failure. The Undo-commissioned survey frames the same underlying shift as a debugging and comprehension problem specific to complex, mission-critical codebases, per InfoQ's reporting. The Harvard-reported findings frame it in economic terms, as a bottleneck that limits how much of AI's coding-speed gains convert into completed software, per InfoQ.
